- Summary

- The U.S. government has launched an ambitious strategy to catalyse the deployment of early-stage fusion power plants by the mid-2030s, but its success hinges on more federal funding, industry leaders say.

August 25 - Fusion developers face steep engineering challenges to transition from experimental physics to commercial net energy gain, such as developing neutron-resistant structural materials that can withstand extreme heat, as well as “breeding blankets” to secure a steady supply of tritium, a nuclear fuel.

The Department of Energy (DOE) Fusion Science and Technology Roadmap released in June is the ​central plank of a federal strategy to help developers overcome these challenges. The Office of Fusion, which was created in November 2025, will coordinate its implementation.

Unlike existing nuclear facilities that rely on fission—the splitting of ‌heavy atoms like uranium—fusion companies are developing first-of-a-kind devices to fuse light atoms together.

This is the same reaction that powers the sun, and while it promises virtually limitless, carbon-free energy, the industry is still in an experimental stage. Private developers have not yet achieved "net energy gain,” the point where a fusion reaction produces more energy than was used to start and sustain it.

DOE's strategy has three main goals: building infrastructure to close fusion materials and technology gaps, facilitating access to AI and advanced computing and research facilities to accelerate innovation, and leveraging public-private partnerships to expand the supply chain and workforce.

It sets ​concrete short, medium and long-term milestones to ensure that fusion companies develop the structural materials, plasma-facing components and fuel processing technology needed to achieve the stated goals.

Many of the 28 U.S. fusion companies are developing tokamaks or stellarators—doughnut-shaped devices ​that use powerful magnetic fields to confine superheated plasma for controlled nuclear fusion research. Others have chosen alternative systems, such as laser-based inertial confinement fusion.

Most fusion concepts “face the same hard problems: separating and reprocessing tritium fuel and building components that can survive a constant barrage of high-energy neutrons,” said Robb Hughes, Head of External Affairs at Realta Fusion.

The roadmap offers a comprehensive pathway to ​commercial deployment, but the goals are only achievable if the government increases funding, he told Reuters Events.

“The plan isn’t the problem. We all agree on the plan. The X factor is the funding. If we underfund the U.S. fusion program and we cut vital scope, we can all watch the timeline to commercialisation slip,” he said.

According to the ​Fusion Industry Association, fusion companies raised a record $4.48 billion worldwide in the 12 months to July 2026 with most of the money coming from private investors. Cumulative funding reported since 2021 rose to $14.24 billion.

The U.S. government funds fusion research through the Fusion Energy Sciences (FES) program.

Legislation approved in 2022 authorised up to $1.1 billion for FES for financial year 2027. However, Congress ultimately appropriated $790 million for next year, below the $805.7 million enacted for 2026.

“All the fusion industry is asking for is alignment between what has been authorised and allocated and what gets enacted,” Hughes said.

The U.S. fusion sector needs more than $1 billion ​a year over the next decade, he said.

Those countries that combine a clear policy framework with sustained support for commercialization will be best positioned to capture the economic and energy security benefits of fusion, a spokesperson for General Fusion told Reuters Events.

**Partnerships, federal labs**

The roadmap seeks to promote public private partnerships (PPPs) between developers and research institutions.

It does so by focusing on existing DOE programs, including Milestone-Based Fusion Development and Innovation Network for Fusion Energy (INFUSE).

The Milestone program, which was allocated a $415 million budget for 2022-2027, reimburses eight companies after they meet pre-negotiated technical targets. These are [Commonwealth Fusion Systems](https://www.reutersevents.com/nuclear/us-army-aims-build-smr-2028-britain-us-sign-pact-accelerate-nuclear "https://www.reutersevents.com/nuclear/us-army-aims-build-smr-2028-britain-us-sign-pact-accelerate-nuclear") (CFS), Focused Energy, Realta Fusion, Thea Energy, Tokamak Energy, Type One Energy, Xcimer Energy, and Zap Energy.

This is the right template as taxpayers only pay for results, Hughes said.

INFUSE gives private fusion companies direct access to expertise, computing power and testing facilities at DOE national laboratories and universities.

Leading fusion ​developers including CFS, Type One Energy, Zap Energy, Pacific Fusion, ​and Realta have also secured INFUSE awards. They often collaborate ⁠with federally funded research labs such as Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, Oak Ridge National Laboratory, and Princeton Plasma Physics Laboratory to carry out fusion experiments.

Collaborations with international researchers and state-funded labs “has been critical to the development of our MTF \[Magnetized Target Fusion\] technology,” the General Fusion spokesperson said.

The DOE also awarded $107 million to six research ​consortia in January 2025 under the FIRE program, which seeks to build collaboration between national laboratories, universities and private companies.

In addition, Fusion BRIDGE, launched earlier this year, will co-fund and ​build shared, small-to-midscale experimental facilities.

Access to national ⁠laboratories “allows private developers to solve common technical challenges faster, reduce development costs, and avoid duplicating expensive infrastructure,” said Amy Roma, partner and Global Nuclear Practice leader at the Orrick, Herrington & Sutcliffe law firm.

“The roadmap’s goals are ambitious but achievable,” she told Reuters Events.

**AI platform to boost R&D**

The roadmap aims to foster the use of AI, advanced computing and quantum technologies to advance fusion deployment.

These tools allow developers to build digital twins of their reactors to stress test components before manufacturing them, and adjust magnetic fields to stabilise plasma shapes, a big challenge in ⁠fusion development.

DOE plans ​to create an AI-Fusion Digital Convergence Platform to connect data from experiments and test facilities with simulations, digital twins and engineering tools.
